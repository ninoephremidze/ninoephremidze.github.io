Lensing visualizations

Gravitational waves passing a dark matter subhalo

Gravitational waves from merging black holes have wavelengths of hundreds to thousands of kilometres. When a lens is comparable in size, the waves do not just bend: they diffract and interfere, leaving a frequency-dependent imprint on the signal.

Change the lens mass to move from diffraction, where the lens is almost invisible, through interference, to geometric optics, where the detector records two copies of the signal.

What you are looking at

The lensed signal is the unlensed one multiplied, frequency by frequency, by the amplification factor F(w, y), with w = 8πG MLz f / c³. Below w ≈ 1 the wavelength is larger than the lens and F ≈ 1. Well above it, F is the sum of two images, the second delayed by Δt, which shows up as regular oscillations in |F(f)| and as beating, then an echo, in the waveform.

F here is the exact point-mass result (Takahashi & Nakamura 2003), evaluated as the geometric-optics sum plus a tabulated wave-optics correction, accurate to better than 1% for most settings. For extended lenses such as NFW or cored halos, F has to be computed numerically, which is what FIONA does efficiently. The waveform is a simple inspiral–merger–ringdown model with the right shape, not a precision template.

The wave field above is a two-dimensional Huygens–Fresnel calculation through a thin gravitational phase screen. It shows the same physics, diffraction and interference behind the lens, but its wavelength and geometry are chosen for display.
